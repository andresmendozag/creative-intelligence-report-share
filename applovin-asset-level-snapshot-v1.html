0.2 Why IPM (not CPI) for the quality tag

We use Derived_IPM (installs ÷ impressions × 1000) — not CPI — to classify quality.
  • Auction alignment: AppLovin bids based on predicted IPM at impression-time. Using IPM aligns our quality measure with the algorithm's optimization target.
  • Non-circularity: CPI = spend ÷ installs. Since we use spend as an outcome (who captures spend), classifying by CPI would create circularity. IPM (installs ÷ impressions) is independent of spend allocation.
  • Statistical bound: IPM is a rate — Wilson confidence intervals fit cleanly. CPI is a ratio of two derived quantities and harder to bound.

0.3 Scale tier — same gate structure across all tiers

TierSpend rank in typeBundle lead rateWhat it means
HEROTop 5%≥ 30%Big spender AND leads ≥ 30% of the bundles it appears in
WINTop 25%≥ 20%Strong spender AND leads enough bundles to matter
CONTRIBUTORTop 50%≥ 10%Mid-spender AND occasionally leads its bundles
PASSENGERAnything not above—Low spend capture OR rarely leads

Bundle lead rate = (# bundles where this asset captures the most reconciled spend) ÷ (# bundles the asset appears in). It is the share of bundles where the asset is the de-facto winner. A creative with high spend rank but very low bundle lead rate is fundamentally different from one that frequently leads — treating them the same would mask the "high spend by accident of bundle placement" pattern. By requiring BOTH conditions at each tier, the gate stays consistent across the whole axis.

How to read: each dot is one asset. x-axis = spend percentile in its asset type — the further left, the bigger the spender. y-axis = bundle lead rate — the share of bundles where this asset is the spend leader. The dotted lines are the gate cutoffs: vertical at 5% / 25% / 50% spend rank, horizontal at 30% / 20% / 10% lead rate. What you see: the green dots in the top-left rectangle are HERO assets that pass both gates (high spend AND high lead rate). The grey cloud in the right half is PASSENGERs — assets that didn't clear the spend gate. Notice the assets in the top-left that AREN'T green: those are high-lead-rate creatives that haven't captured enough spend to be HERO yet. They are the candidates closest to graduating to the next tier.

0.4 Quality tag — IPM band × boolean exposure (action-verb names)

Exposure (boolean): an asset is established if it appears in ≥ 4 bundles within at least one of the 4 campaigns. The per-campaign 90th percentile of bundles-per-asset is 2, so ≥ 4 within a single campaign is a meaningful saturation threshold. ~5% of assets in the portfolio qualify as established under this gate.

TagIPM band (within type)ExposureWhat it means + recommended action
KEEPTop quartileEstablishedReliable winner. Converts well AND saturated in at least one campaign. Hold position in the rotation.
TESTTop quartileNot establishedPromising — needs scale. Converts well BUT under-tested. Promote to more bundles to confirm the signal.
WATCHMid bandEstablishedAverage reliable. Mid-tier converter, tested at scale. Hold in rotation; don't expand exposure.
KILLBottom half≥ 5,000 impressionsRetire from new bundles. Poor converter at a sample size large enough to be confident — the call is robust. Bottom-half-IPM assets with fewer than 5,000 impressions fall to WAIT instead (the estimate is too uncertain to act on).
WAITMid band OR bottom-half-with-low-impressionsNot established OR < 5,000 impressionsNeeds more data. Either a mid-IPM asset that hasn't been seeded in enough bundles to classify, OR a low-IPM asset that hasn't been served enough impressions to retire with confidence. Give it more exposure before deciding.

Why action-verb names: each tag IS the recommended action — readers don't have to translate "PROVEN" into "keep" or "EMERGING" into "promote." Quality is still independent from scale: scale uses spend + bundle lead rate; quality uses IPM + exposure — no shared inputs.

3 · Q1 — Does adding assets to a bundle bring more scale?

The question: bigger bundles might attract more spend because the algorithm gives them more budget — or they might just split the same algorithmic budget over more assets. We need to distinguish "real new scale" from "artificial mirroring."

How to read: blue bars (left axis) = median bundle spend at each asset_count. Red line (right axis) = median bundle IPM. Marker size on the line = median installs per bundle. What you want to see: if spend grows but IPM stays flat as size grows, bigger bundles attract attention without improving efficiency.

Control 2 — Multivariate regression: does asset_count's effect shrink when n_anchors is in the model?

How to read: bars are OLS coefficients with 95% CI. We fit two models: Model A regresses log(cs_spend) on asset_count alone. Model B adds n_anchors as a second predictor. Insight

Adding assets to a bundle does not independently bring scale. The raw coefficient on asset_count is +0.018 (Model A · asset_count alone); when we control for anchor count, it flips to -0.111 while n_anchors picks up a large positive +0.360 (Model B). The 'bigger bundle = more spend' pattern is a confound — what's actually moving the dial is whether the bundle contains anchors.

Operational read: stop sizing bundles up for the sake of size. Two anchors in a 3-asset bundle will outperform six non-anchors in a 7-asset bundle on spend capture.

4 · Q2a — Hypothesis tests A, B, C, D

Four tests of "does adding assets to bundles help?" — designed so the team can defend or refute the practice with statistical evidence. Tests A/B operate on all eligible bundles. Tests C/D specifically leverage the anchor knowledge.

Test A · Does adding assets improve IPM (ALL bundles)?

For each split N ∈ {2,3,4,5,6}: Mann-Whitney U comparing bundle IPM between ≤N vs >N assets.

Test C · Same as A but ONLY in bundles with ≥ 2 anchors

Stratifies to the team's actual pattern (anchor-heavy bundles).

Test A verdict: No statistically significant difference at any N (all p > 0.05). Adding assets to bundles neither helps nor hurts at a detectable level. Test C verdict: Even in bundles with ≥2 anchors, no significant difference at any N. Adding more assets to anchor-heavy bundles doesn't help IPM either.

5 · Q3 — Anchor crowd-out (does anchor presence prevent non-anchors from winning?)

Hypothesis: when anchor assets are present in a bundle, do they crowd out non-anchor assets from winning the top-1 spend slot — even after controlling for the structural fact that anchors take up slots in the bundle? Three complementary views:
  • Q3.1 stratifies the raw rate: in cells with N anchors × M non-anchors, what % of bundles produce a non-anchor top-1?
  • Q3.2 formalizes this with a logistic regression that controls for non-anchor count.
  • Q3.3 shifts framing from "who wins top-1" to "how much spend do anchors capture" — the algorithm-favors-familiar view.

Q3.1 — Stratified sanity-check: where do non-anchors actually win?

How to read: each cell shows the % of bundles in that (non-anchor count × anchor count) combination where a non-anchor asset won the top-1 reconciled-spend slot. Green = non-anchors winning more often; red = anchors dominating. What to look for: reading across a row (fix non-anchor count), if the % drops sharply as anchor count grows, that's the crowd-out signal in the raw data. Q3.2 — Logistic regression: anchor crowd-out, controlled for non-anchor count

P(top-1 is non-anchor) = logit⁻¹(β₀ + β₁·n_anchors + β₂·n_non_anchors)

How to read (v1, pooled): bars are log-odds coefficients with 95% CI; annotations show OR for a one-unit change. Q3.2 v2 — Type-aware top-1 models (the right way to ask "do anchors help non-anchors?")

Why disaggregate. The team's strategy specifically pairs non-anchor videos with anchor HTMLs (Q4.1 shows 42% of eligible bundles are exactly this composition). The pooled Q3.2 v1 positive coefficient does not survive type disaggregation.   • Max 3 anchors of the same type per bundle (Q3.4 — per-anchor SoV falls below cohort-adjusted fair share past n=3; bundle spend collapses past n=2 within a type; IPM peaks at n=3 then drops).
  • Do NOT impose a blanket hard total-anchor cap from this evidence alone. Q3.5 supports cross-type complementarity for SPEND in the observed unbalanced cells (1V×4+H, 4+V×1H, etc. — significantly higher than baseline in the Q3.5b categorical model); the IPM evidence is weaker and the main effects of n_video_anchors and n_html_anchors on log(IPM) are individually negative. Balanced moderate cells like 2V×2H, 3V×2H, 2V×3H, and 3V×3H are sparse or unobserved in the data (n<10 each). Keep the per-type cap at 3; treat balanced multi-anchor configurations as controlled test cells, not default deployments. If the team wants to deploy a 3V×3H, run it as a small experimental cohort and re-evaluate against this baseline.
  • Reserved TEST slots in each exploration bundle (Q3.3 — anchors take ~50% of bundle spend regardless of how many non-anchors are present; without reserved slots, TEST candidates remain starving).

Open questions this report cannot close: (1) whether anchors actively scaffold non-anchor creatives — Q4 lacks the pure-non-anchor counterfactual, and Q3.2 v2's type-aware models actually find the reverse pattern (more non-anchor videos help anchor HTMLs win, not vice versa); (2) what drives the genuine IPM lift at n=3 same-type anchors (D2 anomaly); (3) whether the marginal contribution of any specific asset is meaningfully different in a context-matched comparison (requires a matched-bundles or propensity-scored analysis beyond this snapshot). All three belong on the open-investigations list for Report B's time-series view.
